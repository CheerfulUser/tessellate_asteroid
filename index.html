About the catalogue

TESS observes a 24°×96° strip of sky continuously for roughly a month at a time. Asteroids drift through that field, and the TESSELLATE reduction extracts their photometry from the full-frame images. The result is unusually long, uninterrupted coverage: baselines of weeks to months, spanning hundreds of rotations, where ground-based photometry is broken by daylight and weather.

That matters most for two things. Slow rotators whose period exceeds a single night are recoverable here. And because the baseline covers so many cycles, the period itself is pinned far more tightly — for (75) Eurydike, to roughly 25 seconds over 1,751 rotations.

Data products

Phase-folded lightcurve
Stacked photometry folded at the adopted period and binned, with the standard error on each bin. Plotted on every object page and downloadable as JSON.
Full stacked lightcurve
The complete per-epoch photometry behind the fold, geometry-corrected and normalised per visit. Gzipped CSV, for anyone refitting the period or modelling the object independently.
Convex shape model
Derived by convex inversion (DAMIT convexinv) at 289 facets. Rotatable in the browser, synchronised to the lightcurve, and downloadable as a JSON mesh.

How to read a shape model

Most of these asteroids were observed during a single apparition, from one viewing direction. That constrains the shape but not its orientation in space: running the inversion from different starting poles returns solutions scattered by a hundred degrees or more. Every object page reports that scatter as the pole spread, and it should be read before trusting an orientation. The rotation period, by contrast, is well determined.

Relationship to the LCDB

Where an asteroid already has a published period in the Asteroid Lightcurve Database, both values are shown. Agreement is common; where the two differ it is usually the double-peaked ambiguity, since an elongated body shows two maxima per rotation and a periodogram can lock onto either. A smaller number are cases where the literature period leaves no trace in the TESS photometry at all.
